What No Man Can Find Out | Ecclesiastes 7:19-8:17

person Justin Perdue
view_list Ecclesiastes | Life Under the Sun , 

Solomon’s exhaustive search for wisdom keeps running into the same wall: sin is universal, human power is often abused, and the righteous and wicked don’t always get what they deserve. His conclusion, “under the sun,” is stark — no one, however wise, can fully decode how life or God’s providence works. But viewed “above the sun,” God’s delay in judgment is kindness meant to lead people to repentance, and His sovereign purposes are never derailed by human evil. We won’t be given full answers to life’s hardest questions — instead, we’re called to rest not in our own righteousness or understanding, but in Christ, by faith.

What does he say? He talks about what he will do. Okay, but here’s the reality. God delays his justice so that he might save his people through Christ, and yet fallen humanity continually abuses that, abuses his patience.

Rather than agreeing with God and turning from our sin and pride to Christ and faith, we think, well, I’m not facing judgment. People get away with evil all the time. Apparently, I can just keep doing whatever, or I’ve got time. I’ll get right with God eventually.

Friend, if you sit here today, don’t be a fool. Whoever is wise, let him consider the word and the love of the Lord. God’s patience with you is not evidence that he condones your sin. God’s patience with you up to now is no guarantee that he will give you longer.

His kindness is meant to lead you to repentance. Turn from yourself. Turn from your pride and your sin, and turn to Jesus in faith.

To everybody sitting here, including myself, consider this, Jesus is the only one who can do anything about your corruption, about your sinful record, and your lack of righteousness. If you keep following Paul in Romans, after those early verses of Romans 2 that I just talked through and read, God is a just and impartial judge. He will reward those who do good. He will punish those who do evil.

Those who patiently seek glory, honor, and immortality, he will reward. But there is a problem, according to Paul. There is no one good. No one seeks for God.

There is none righteous, no, not one. We need a righteousness that is not our own, which is exactly what the gospel reveals. The righteousness that God gives to sinners, apart from the law, apart from our ability to keep it, God gives righteousness to sinners through faith in Jesus Christ, and it is for all who believe. It can’t be by our works.

All have sinned, all have broken God’s law, all have fallen short of the glory of God. It is only through faith in Jesus Christ that anyone is ever forgiven, declared righteous, or promised eternal life. There is no other way.

So trust him today. Take hold on him today. Christ is yours. Receive him.

And you are his. Rest in him.

God Has Declared the End from the Beginning

Next piece that I want us to think about together. This is an above the sun perspective. God has declared the end from the beginning.

So God is all wise and all knowing. Amen, yes? But that’s not all that the scripture reveals about him. He governs all things according to his wise and holy providence.

He knows the future, not because he’s really smart, but because he has planned the future. He has declared the end from the beginning, and from ancient times, things not yet done. He says that he accomplishes all of his purposes, that his counsel always stands, and that no one can stop him from accomplishing what he means to accomplish. And he incorporates into his wise and holy providence even the sinful actions and intentions of human beings.

Break your brain. Scripture is explicit and clear about human responsibility, human culpability, human agency. We reason, we desire, we make choices. Those choices matter.

We are accountable. And God’s purposes are never thwarted. How all of this hangs together in every detail is not ours to know. It’s beyond us.

The secret things belong to the Lord. What has been revealed belongs to us and our children. But there are some things we can say, these revealed things, and these should encourage our souls. It is not just that God takes bad things.

Here’s where I’m going, ’cause sometimes you hear people talking to each other, Christians, well-intended, largely true. People are going through stuff, and they look at each other and say, “Hey, you know, God’s gonna work it for good.” It’s gonna turn out for good. It’s not just that God takes bad things, bad decisions, wicked intentions, evil schemes, and then somehow works them for good afterward. You tracking?

It’s not that he takes all these bad things and works them for good after the fact. Scripture says something much stronger. Through the evil intentions, plans, and schemes of human beings, God means to accomplish good, and he does.

Genesis 50, 20 is an obvious example. Joseph’s brothers meant him evil and harm. They acted out of envy, jealousy, hatred, malice, all that. What is the word that Joseph speaks to?

As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good. You meant evil, he meant good. God was preserving a people in Egypt. Remarkable, but the even better example, many of you know where I’m going, is the crucifixion of Jesus Christ.

Acts chapter two, Acts chapter four. In those places, we read that wicked people did what wicked people wanted to do. Wicked people had wicked motivations and acted out of them. At the very same time, God was purposefully saving the world through Christ.

Human beings meant malice, hatred, envy, murder, death. God meant salvation. Marvel at that. Marvel at that.

Humans mean one thing. God has no fellowship with anybody in their sin, nor is he the author of evil, but through those evil, wicked intentions, God means salvation and he accomplishes it. Give him praise and honor and glory for that.

Here’s something really important though. We cannot see how both things are true in the moment. You feel me? We can’t see how both are true in the moment.

This is very instructive for our hearts, for our lives. We cannot see how human evil and the good purposes of God are hanging together while the thing is happening.

Think about the disciples when Jesus was crucified. They couldn’t see it. They didn’t understand everything God was doing. I mean, I don’t even know that they understood the first thing that he was doing.

From where they stood, their Messiah had been arrested, condemned, beaten, crucified, and buried. They couldn’t see salvation being accomplished through this even though Jesus had told them repeatedly. That it would, but couldn’t see when it was happening. And you and I, we are just like that.

We can’t see it when it’s happening. There is much of the work of God we don’t or can’t see.

Do Not Become Job’s Friends

I’m pivoting slightly here. I don’t wanna snap anybody’s neck too hard, okay? ‘Cause we’re going in a different direction. Same truth, different application. In our text today and throughout Ecclesiastes, we’ve seen that part of the problem the preacher’s grappling with is the reality of human sin.

But that’s only part of the problem. The preacher is also having an impossible time making sense of God’s providential rule over a fallen and broken creation.

You can put your eyes back on chapter seven and verse 13 so that you don’t think that I’m misquoting the Bible. Consider the work of God. Who can make straight what he has made crooked? When Solomon says that God has made things crooked, he isn’t saying that God originally made creation evil.

God made all things good. Sin is what has wrecked it all. What he’s referring to is the fact that God has allowed the broken order of the world and all of the frustration and exasperation and the wickedness to remain. And this order doesn’t change.

There’s nothing new under the sun. No matter how much we try to change it, we can’t. That’s what Solomon is referring to. God lets things remain as they are for now.

He does reign sovereignly and providentially over it, but he does so in a way that is beyond us. We are not privy to everything he’s doing. And so we have a really hard time assessing suffering and pain and injustice and a whole host of other things that we encounter in this life. Our vantage point is quite limited.

But that does not keep us from getting out over our skis and making pronouncements that we ought never make. So this is another admonition to us all. My pastoral word of caution to myself and to us, do not become Job’s friends. Do not become Job’s friends.

We are prone to take general principles scripture gives us and over apply them without taking the entire witness of scripture into account. Think about Job’s friends. You know the situation. I don’t have time to give you the backfill.

Job wasn’t suffering because of some particular sin that he committed. God was doing something. Job’s friends, they sit with him for a little while and then they start talking. And as soon as they open their mouths, things start to go downhill quickly.

Their reasoning, we’ve talked about these things before. It’s like, look bro, you’re suffering so you must have sinned. God is just. You had to do something to deserve this.

This is divine retribution, bro. You need to search your heart. You need to find the sin. You need to root it out and confess it.

And then maybe things will get better. How many pastors and Christians think and talk this way? Somebody is suffering, life isn’t going well and the counsel is search your heart. Identify that hidden sin.

Root it out and the suffering may very well end. That kind of counsel is often completely unhelpful.

That kind of counsel is often completely unhelpful and can do a lot of damage to another believer. Because it may not be the case at all. We’re not privy to the hidden will of God. We’re not privy to all the things that God is doing in the lives of each of his people.

We might see one or two out of the thousands of things that he is doing in the lives of each of his saints. We don’t know.

Think about Paul. He does not interpret his sufferings as evidence that he has failed, as evidence that he has sinned, or that God is paying him back for some particular wrong. He understands suffering as part and parcel of being united to Christ, of being called by Christ, and of living in a fallen world that is hostile to Christ. His suffering, you know what he wrote, becomes a setting in which the grace and power of Jesus are made manifest.

John chapter nine gives us the same thing. Who sinned, Jesus? This man or his parents that he was born blind? Jesus rejects the whole premise, neither actually.

It’s so that the works of God might be displayed in him. There was no hidden sin in that blind man’s life for him to root out and confess. God was doing something. I’m gonna let that sit.

Don’t hear what I’m not saying. Don’t hear what I’m not saying. So, brother, what you’re telling me is that we should just never counsel each other, we should never correct anybody, we should never rebuke, we should never talk like God’s law does. Absurd.

That’s not what I mean at all. We can and should hold out God’s law as the perfect guide for all of our lives. God uses his law and the lives of his saints to convict us of sin, to show us the depth of our need, to humble us, to drive us yet again to Christ. Pursuing what God calls good is good.

Encourage each other in that. Please, encourage me in that. Fleeing from what God calls sin is good. Exhort each other like that.

Exhort me. We should exhort, admonish, and encourage one another. May that never be a question. But we should not look at somebody who is suffering and presume to know the cause and then make very specific pronouncements as to why.

Because we are not privy to that information. This is in italics in my notes. You do that what you will.

We can be clear about what God has revealed in his law while remaining humble about what he has not revealed in his hidden will in providence. Make sense? Clear about what he’s revealed in his law, humble as it pertains to what he has not when it comes to his providence and his hidden will. That gives us perspective.

We should be slower to speak. We should be thoughtful when we do. This sermon has been very long. I’m about to land it, and you’re all thankful.

Final Thoughts: The Troubles of This Life Will Not Have the Last Word

Final thoughts. The troubles of this life will not have the last word. Amen, someone? The troubles of this life will not have the last word.

There are senseless things that happen in this life, to be sure. There are things that are not as they should be, no doubt. That can, we all know. It can discourage us.

It can lead us to despair, frustration, anxiety, depression, all of it. Solomon has poured his heart out about all of this, and we resonate with him. But none of these things will have the final say viewed above the sun because of God and God alone. We can even say above the sun that the troubles and trials and sufferings of this life for the believer, far from being pointless, actually serve God’s purposes.

Is that not a welcome word in light of Ecclesiastes? Everything’s vanity. Everything’s futility. Everything’s senseless.

Not if you’re in Christ. We can even count it joy when we meet trials of various kinds, says James.

Now, that’s not because trials are good. They’re not. That’s not because suffering is good. It’s not.

The calamity and the suffering and the sickness itself is terrible. But what God does through these things in the lives of his children, now that’s marvelous. That’s supernatural. Grace all in it.

And most notably, what does he do? He works steadfastness in his children. We continue to trust him. We continue to trust Christ in spite of all the experiences that might seem to give us reasons to do the opposite.

And that preaches a word to us. We’ve said before, not original to me, we need to rip our eyes out and put ’em in our ears. What I mean by that is don’t live by what you see. We don’t live by sight.

We live by faith, according to the word of God. Life in this world often presents us with very messy and unclear pictures. But from God’s perspective, nothing is unclear.

Now, the thing is, the hard part for us is that he doesn’t often give us that perspective. We’re not afforded that. He gives us glimpses sometimes, and we thank him. But ultimately, we live by faith.

We take him at his word. We trust that our trials and sufferings have a purpose even when we can’t explain them. And that doesn’t mean that we deny the hardness of suffering. It doesn’t mean that we just throw platitudes around.

I wanna reiterate something that we’ve considered before. If you or I look at our lives, our circumstances, our sufferings, our trials, our pain, we will often struggle to find evidence that God is for us. He’s promised us what the end will be. He’s told us how he loves us.

He has told us how he sees us in his Son. But we will struggle to see that if we are always looking at our lives and our circumstances.

So put this on your refrigerator if you’re that kind of person. The evidence of God’s love, grace, and mercy toward us is not our lives or circumstances. It is Jesus for us. Jesus, who he is, what he did, and his heart toward us is the evidence that God is for us.

How great is God’s love? To what length has he gone to save? Look at Christ. What’s God like?

Is he compassionate? Is he gentle? Is he merciful? Look at Christ.

As has been said, we don’t have answers for skin, knees, and cancers. But we do have a Savior that bears them with us. It’s a scandalous message, you know. We have a God who bleeds, took on flesh and bled to save his people.

Look at that to know God is for you. Because like Solomon, we live under the sun. We’re gonna keep seeing the way things are. We’re gonna have many of these same reactions that he does.

The preacher’s final answer is not that the world has no God or that the world has no truth. It’s that life under the sun cannot be mastered. It can’t be decoded. It can’t be made safe by human wisdom.

His conclusion is that we do not know the whole work of God. And so our conclusion is that we live by faith. We receive the good gifts that he gives. We don’t hope in circumstances.

We don’t lean on our own understanding. We hope, we trust, we rest in Jesus Christ who loved us and gave himself for us. This is where we pillow our head.

This is why we can go to sleep in peace. And now we close in prayer.
